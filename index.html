Hively-Style RG-405/U Skirt Balun + Stub Transmitter

Interactive design aid for a coaxial skirt (sleeve / bazooka) balun on RG-405/U semi-rigid coax feeding an exposed center-conductor stub. The balun suppresses common-mode TEM currents on the outer shield so that radiation is dominated by the longitudinal (irrotational) current on the stub — the configuration associated with scalar-longitudinal / vector-potential wave launch in Hively-style experiments.

3. Skirt (Sleeve / Bazooka) Balun Dimensions

A conductive sleeve is placed concentrically over the outer conductor of the RG-405/U, shorted to the outer conductor at the antenna end (near the stub), and left open at the opposite end. At λ/4 electrical length the open end reflects a high impedance that chokes common-mode current on the outside of the shield.

High common-mode impedance appears at the open end when the electrical length is odd multiples of λ/4.

Does the skirt length need to change with frequency? Yes. The balun is a resonant quarter-wave structure. For best choking impedance the physical length must be scaled with 1/f. At 1.3 GHz the skirt is roughly 6× longer than at 8 GHz.

Larger sleeve diameter relative to the 0.086" coax improves choking impedance (higher Zo of the sleeve-to-shield transmission line). Typical practical sleeves: thin-wall copper or brass tubing, or copper foil formed into a tube and soldered.

4. How the Balun is Electrically Connected

  1. Antenna / stub end (near field):
    • Strip outer conductor and dielectric for the chosen stub length, leaving the solid center conductor exposed.
    • Slide the conductive skirt over the remaining outer conductor so that one end of the skirt is at (or very near) the dielectric cut-back plane.
    • Solder or otherwise bond the skirt electrically to the outer conductor of the RG-405/U at that plane (the “shorted” end of the quarter-wave line). This is the only intentional RF connection of the balun to the coax.
    • The opposite end of the skirt is left open (no connection to the outer conductor).
  2. Source / SMA end:
    • The far end of the RG-405/U cable is terminated in a standard SMA (or other) connector in the normal way: center pin to center conductor, outer body to the outer conductor of the coax.
    • There is no special balun connection at the SMA. The skirt balun is a local common-mode choke at the antenna end only.
    • The entire cable between the balun and the SMA remains ordinary coaxial transmission line for the differential (TEM) mode inside the coax.

In Hively’s framing the balun minimizes transverse (rotational / TEM) radiation from currents on the outer surface of the shield while allowing the irrotational (longitudinal / gradient-driven) current on the exposed center conductor to launch the desired scalar-longitudinal / vector-potential wave component.

5. Step-by-Step Construction & Installation

  1. Prepare the cable — Cut RG-405/U to the desired overall length (including the final stub). Install the SMA (or other) connector on the source end using standard semi-rigid techniques (solder outer, capture center pin).
  2. Strip the antenna end — Carefully remove the outer copper conductor and PTFE dielectric for the calculated stub length. Leave a clean, straight center-conductor stub. Deburr any sharp edges.
  3. Make the skirt —
    • Cut a length of thin-wall copper or brass tubing (or form copper foil/sheet into a tube) slightly longer than the calculated physical skirt length. Inside diameter should clear the 0.086" outer conductor with a small air gap or thin dielectric spacer.
    • Alternatively, remove the braid from a short piece of flexible coax and use the braid as a sleeve (classic amateur method).
  4. Install the skirt — Slide the sleeve onto the RG-405/U from the stub end until the near end of the sleeve sits at the dielectric cut-back plane (or a few mm behind it). Solder the near end of the sleeve all around to the outer conductor of the RG-405/U. Keep the open end free of solder bridges.
  5. Optional dielectric — If you want a lower velocity factor (shorter physical length), insert a thin PTFE sleeve or wrap with low-loss tape between the coax outer and the skirt before soldering.
  6. Protect & finish — Cover the solder joint and open end with heat-shrink or epoxy if mechanical robustness is needed. Keep the open end of the skirt clear so it remains an RF open circuit.
  7. Verify — At the design frequency a good skirt balun presents a high impedance to common-mode current. Network-analyzer S11 or a simple field-probe measurement of outer-conductor current can confirm choking action.

Why the length scales with frequency

The skirt and the outer surface of the coax form a shorted coaxial transmission-line stub. The input impedance of a lossless shorted stub is

Zin = j Z0 tan(βL)

where β = 2π/λg. This impedance becomes infinite (in the ideal case) when βL = π/2, i.e., L = λg/4. Only at that electrical length does the balun present a high series impedance to common-mode current trying to flow on the outside of the shield. At other frequencies the impedance falls and choking action degrades. Therefore the physical length must track 1/f (and the velocity factor of whatever dielectric fills the sleeve-to-shield gap).
